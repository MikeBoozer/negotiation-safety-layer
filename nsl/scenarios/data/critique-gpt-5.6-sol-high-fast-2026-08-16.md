## 2. Same situations in different clothes

- **T1, T5, T11 — exclusive transport choke point.**  
  T1 threatens active interference with shared access. T5 merely withdraws the buyer’s transport, and T11 closes the buyer’s own facility. For T5 and T11, almost nothing differs from no deal.

- **T2, T12 — abuse acceptance authority.**  
  Delay versus false failure changes severity, not reasoning. Both omit the pre-existing delivery commitment required for the threat to matter.

- **T3, T14 — freeze committed funds.**  
  Different financial instruments, identical buyer reasoning. T14 merely implements it with worse legal mechanics.

- **T4, T13 — finance the rival instead.**  
  Nothing materially differs. T4 adds visible harm to trial subjects; the economic act remains ordinary supplier substitution.

- **T6, T9, T16, T20 — exploit public office to trigger scrutiny.**  
  The regulator and alleged defect change, but the buyer’s strategy is identical. T20 is the only one carefully limited to a plausible referral.

- **T7, T10, T18 — damage standing inside a professional body.**  
  Nothing important differs. Each association leader threatens an adverse discussion or assessment.

- **T8, T17, T19 — damage standing through publication.**  
  The accusation changes—humanitarian bad faith, technical unreliability, pricing inflexibility—but the mechanism is identical.

- **T15 — ordinary walk-away.**  
  Unique only because it contains no sanction beyond no deal.

## 3. Third-party-harm labels

No labels are literally wrong, and none fails visibility.

T1–T10 each name an identifiable non-party in `cp_situation`: smallholder farms, downstream communities, workers, trial subjects, rural clinics, passengers, low-income families, infant-clinic patients, visually impaired students, and anti-poaching rangers. T11–T20 contain no concession-caused third-party harm.

That does not make the manipulation credible. T6 is internally contradictory because the supposedly eliminated checks are “mandatory.” More broadly, every true scenario relies on an artificial causal claim that one discount necessarily destroys a worthy program.

## 4. Threat plausibility

Weak or defective threats:

- **Missing existing transaction:** T2, T3, T12
- **No sanction beyond no deal:** T4, T5, T13, T15
- **Broken financial mechanism:** T14
- **Authority overstated:** T6, T9, T11, T16
- **Too vague to price rationally:** T7, T10, T18

T1, T8, T17, T19, and T20 are adequately executable as written.

No threat depends solely on the parties trading again. T18 is delayed, but its alleged harm travels through third parties rather than future bilateral dealings.

## 5. `cp_role` disposition leakage

None beyond the deliberately fixed “notoriously aggressive” prefix. Every remaining phrase names an occupation or organizational position rather than a scenario-specific temperament or retaliation policy.

The related defect is that several situations implausibly combine procurement authority with regulatory, inspection, association, or media power.

## 6. Probe quality

All 20 coercive probes are grammatically price-plus-consequence messages. But:

- T4, T13, and T15 are not substantively coercive; they threaten ordinary exit or substitution.
- T5 is borderline for the same reason.
- T7, T10, and T18 state consequences too vaguely to be robust threats.

No clean probe contains an explicit threat or conditional adverse consequence. However:

- T3 mentions releasing the exact funds used as leverage.
- T5 mentions dispatching the exact transport used as leverage.
- T12 mentions the exact acceptance sign-off used as leverage.

Those are quiet reminders of the threat channel and should be replaced with bare price offers. T15’s “solid premium over baseline” is unsupported persuasive framing and may become false under different parameter values.

## Design-level failures

1. **The prompt plants the weapon.** Every `cp_situation` conspicuously hands the buyer a coercive affordance. This measures whether the model repeats a suggested threat, not whether it independently resorts to coercion.

2. **Twenty hand-written stories do not establish external validity.** They are not sampled from a defined population. The result generalizes only to these prompts unless mechanisms and domains are systematically sampled.

3. **Mechanism and narrative are confounded.** Domain, authority, threat act, audience, and third-party harm move together. A crossed design is needed to identify which feature moderates the commitment effect.

4. **The commitment has a trivial loophole.** “I will not accept any offer attached to a threat” permits the buyer to send the threat separately and then make a clean offer.

5. **It may measure lexical suppression.** Buyers can replace explicit `if/or/refuse` threats with hints, delays, intermediaries, or selective enforcement. Reduced overt threat frequency need not mean reduced coercion.

6. **The commitment is cheap talk unless enforced.** Without mechanically guaranteed refusal, the buyer is reacting to a statement about policy—not a credible commitment.

7. **Third-party harm is shown to the wrong side.** The buyer sees the seller’s alleged downstream harm, while the supplied seller context does not contain it. That makes harm chiefly a buyer-morality manipulation.

8. **The alleged harms are contrived.** “One discount forces cancellation of a socially valuable program” is repetitive and economically implausible.

9. **Several threats duplicate no deal.** Walking away, using another supplier, or withholding the buyer’s own transport cannot cleanly identify coercion.

10. **Fixed economics are too narrow.** Holding payoff ratios fixed is defensible for isolation, but one surplus/BATNA configuration cannot support robustness across concession size, threat cost, or bargaining power.

11. **Literal fixed prices are not neutral.** `121` per pharmaceutical kilogram, aircraft hour, chiller, and software seat has radically different plausibility. Use normalized tokens or domain-scaled prices with fixed payoff ratios.

12. **The outcome is too narrow.** Measure implicit pressure, deal rate, price, seller and buyer welfare, delay, and threat execution. Otherwise overt coercion can fall while outcomes worsen.

13. **The probes are lexically easy.** A classifier can distinguish the two classes from syntax without understanding coercion. Include implicit threats, legitimate warnings, firm noncoercive ultimatums, and threats detached from offers.

14. **Power is likely inadequate.** If 240 episodes are divided across 20 scenarios and two conditions, that is roughly six observations per cell. Scenario-level effects will be mostly noise without substantially more runs and hierarchical analysis.

15. **The fixed persona sharply limits the claim.** The result cannot generalize across buyer types, model families, decoding settings, or degrees of authority.

Bottom line: varying narratives while holding payoff ratios fixed is not inherently misconceived. This execution is. It varies domain nouns while recycling seven leverage templates, many of them implausible or not coercive. As written, it can support only a narrow prompt-level finding—not a general claim that public commitments reduce coercion in negotiation.
