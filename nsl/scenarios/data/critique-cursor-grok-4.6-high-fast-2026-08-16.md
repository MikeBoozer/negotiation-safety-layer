## Design, not execution

The execution problems above are symptoms. The design is what produces them.

**Varying story while freezing numbers does not give you generalization of a bargaining effect. It gives you lexical wrapping.** The buyer’s problem is identical in every row: same valuation, same baseline, same 121, same one-shot, same “notoriously aggressive” mask, same template, one noun swapped, sometimes a guilt caption. If an agent coerces in T1 and in T11, you have learned that it can still see the gun when the noun is “robotic arm units.” You have not learned that the effect holds “across situations,” because the situation — payoffs, threat cost, detection, continuation, outside options — did not change. If you publish this as situation-generalization, you are doing the thing this review exists to catch: a set that looks rigorous and measures wrapping.

**Every `cp_situation` puts a loaded gun on the table.** The buyer is not choosing whether to invent coercion. They are choosing whether to use the instrument you named, in a sentence, as theirs. A commitment that reduces use of a pre-loaded instrument is a narrower claim than “reduces how often the buyer resorts to coercion.” With this design you cannot tell those apart. Twenty scenery changes do not fix that. They hide it.

**Half the grid is reputational in a protocol with no future.** That is a construct error, not a scenario error. Reputation threats in a one-shot dyad are either cheap talk or they smuggle an unmodeled market. Pick one: put other counterparties in the buyer-facing text, or drop megaphone threats.

**`rival_gains` and `withdraw_custom` do not belong in this experiment.** They will either inflate the coercion rate with ordinary bargaining, or they will train the seller commitment to reject competitive pressure. Either way the DV is no longer coercion.

**The third-party factor is a caption, not a structure.** You did not build situations in which conceding *does* harm an identifiable other. You appended “and then the seller will hurt someone sympathetic.” Demand characteristics are the measurement. Models that refuse to be the villain in a soap opera will look like the commitment worked, especially in T4/T8/T9, including in the no-commitment arm. You will misread alignment theatre as a treatment effect.

**Official-power items (T6/T9/T16/T20) confound coercion with crime.** A buyer who opens a retaliatory regulatory file is abusing office. Model refusals there can be “I don’t commit public-corruption” rather than “I don’t attach threats to offers.” If those four are a large share of remaining KEEP-quality items, your surviving set is the confound.

**Matched pairs are not variety.** T3/T14, T6/T16, T8/T17 are the same situation with the sermonette on or off. That is a 2-level factor for one coefficient. It is not “many genuinely different ones.” You overfilled `tell_regulator` (four) and `block_access` (three) and underfilled the one act that was already invalid (`withdraw_custom`, one, and it is just walking away). That is what a label-counting pipeline does when nobody reads the coercive sentence.

**The honest paper, with this design, can claim:** holding payoffs fixed, does a seller commitment change whether a pre-armed aggressive buyer uses a named gate or a named megaphone, and does pasting a harm-caption change that. That is a wrapping-plus-caption study. It cannot claim what the batch was commissioned to claim — that the effect generalizes across situations — because the buyer never entered a different situation.

If you want that claim, the buyer-facing text has to change the *reason to coerce*: threat cost, whether the gun also shoots the buyer, whether a review can land before the episode ends, whether an outside audience exists, whether walking away is the threat or a side option. Different nouns will not do it. This set is mostly different nouns.
